# Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital - Thyroid Disorders Knowledge Document

## Overview

The thyroid is a small butterfly-shaped gland located in the front of the neck just below the Adam's apple. Despite its small size, it plays a critical role in regulating metabolism, energy production, body temperature, heart rate, and growth and development through the production of thyroid hormones. The two main hormones produced are triiodothyronine (T3) and thyroxine (T4). The production of these hormones is regulated by thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) produced by the pituitary gland in the brain.

Thyroid disorders occur when the thyroid produces too much hormone (hyperthyroidism), too little hormone (hypothyroidism), or develops structural abnormalities such as nodules or enlargement (goiter). Thyroid disorders are among the most common endocrine conditions worldwide. In India, iodine deficiency has historically been a major cause, though autoimmune thyroid disease is now the leading cause. Women are significantly more affected than men, with thyroid disorders affecting approximately one in eight women during their lifetime.

## Causes

Hypothyroidism, or underactive thyroid, is most commonly caused by Hashimoto's thyroiditis, an autoimmune condition where the immune system attacks thyroid tissue. Other causes include iodine deficiency, previous thyroid surgery or radioactive iodine treatment, certain medications including lithium and amiodarone, pituitary gland disorders, and congenital thyroid abnormalities.

Hyperthyroidism, or overactive thyroid, is most commonly caused by Graves' disease, an autoimmune condition where antibodies stimulate the thyroid to produce excess hormones. Other causes include toxic multinodular goiter, toxic adenoma (a single overactive nodule), thyroiditis (inflammation of the thyroid), excessive iodine intake, and excessive thyroid hormone medication.

Thyroid nodules and goiter can result from iodine deficiency, autoimmune disease, genetic factors, and rarely thyroid cancer.

## Risk Factors

- Female gender with women 5 to 8 times more likely to develop thyroid disorders
- Family history of thyroid disease or autoimmune conditions
- Personal history of autoimmune diseases like Type 1 diabetes or rheumatoid arthritis
- Age above 60 years
- Iodine deficiency or excessive iodine intake
- Previous thyroid surgery or radiation therapy to the neck
- Pregnancy and postpartum period
- Use of certain medications including lithium and amiodarone
- Down syndrome or Turner syndrome
- Previous history of thyroid disorders

## Symptoms

**Hypothyroidism Symptoms**: Fatigue and low energy, unexplained weight gain, cold intolerance, dry skin and hair, hair loss, constipation, depression and mood changes, slow heart rate, menstrual irregularities with heavy periods, muscle weakness and joint pain, puffy face and swollen eyelids, hoarse voice, and memory problems.

**Hyperthyroidism Symptoms**: Unintentional weight loss despite normal or increased appetite, heat intolerance and excessive sweating, rapid or irregular heartbeat and palpitations, anxiety, nervousness, and irritability, tremor in hands and fingers, difficulty sleeping, frequent bowel movements, menstrual irregularities with light or absent periods, bulging eyes in Graves' disease, fatigue and muscle weakness, and increased appetite.

**Thyroid Nodule Symptoms**: Visible or palpable lump in the neck, difficulty swallowing or breathing if nodule is large, hoarse voice, and pain in the neck or throat area.

## Diagnosis

Thyroid function is assessed through blood tests measuring TSH, free T4, and free T3 levels. TSH is the most sensitive screening test. In primary hypothyroidism, TSH is elevated while free T4 is low. In hyperthyroidism, TSH is suppressed while free T4 and T3 are elevated. Subclinical thyroid disease shows abnormal TSH with normal T4 and T3 levels.

Thyroid antibody tests including anti-TPO (thyroid peroxidase antibodies) and anti-thyroglobulin antibodies help diagnose autoimmune thyroid conditions. Thyroid ultrasound evaluates gland size, texture, and presence of nodules. Fine-needle aspiration biopsy is performed on suspicious nodules to rule out thyroid cancer. Radioactive iodine uptake scan differentiates causes of hyperthyroidism. The NABL-certified laboratory at Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital provides comprehensive thyroid testing with accurate and rapid results.

## Treatment Options

**Hypothyroidism Treatment**: Lifelong thyroid hormone replacement with levothyroxine (synthetic T4). The dose is individualized based on TSH levels and adjusted every 6 to 8 weeks until the target TSH is achieved. Regular monitoring every 6 to 12 months ensures adequate dosing.

**Hyperthyroidism Treatment**: Antithyroid medications (Methimazole, Propylthiouracil) reduce thyroid hormone production. Radioactive iodine therapy destroys overactive thyroid tissue, often resulting in hypothyroidism requiring lifelong replacement. Surgery (thyroidectomy) is indicated for large goiters, suspicious nodules, or when other treatments are not suitable. Beta-blockers provide symptomatic relief from rapid heart rate, tremor, and anxiety while definitive treatment takes effect.

**Thyroid Nodule Management**: Benign nodules are monitored with periodic ultrasound. Suspicious or malignant nodules require surgical removal. Thyroid cancer treatment typically involves surgery followed by radioactive iodine therapy and lifelong thyroid hormone suppression.

## Medications Commonly Used

- **Levothyroxine**: The standard treatment for hypothyroidism. Take on an empty stomach 30 to 60 minutes before breakfast. Avoid calcium, iron supplements, and antacids within 4 hours of taking levothyroxine.
- **Methimazole**: First-line antithyroid medication for hyperthyroidism. Requires regular blood count monitoring.
- **Propylthiouracil**: Used in specific situations including first trimester of pregnancy.
- **Beta-Blockers (Propranolol, Atenolol)**: Control symptoms of hyperthyroidism like rapid heart rate and tremor.
- **Calcium and Vitamin D Supplements**: Important for patients with hypothyroidism and post-thyroidectomy patients.

## Lifestyle Management

Take thyroid medication consistently at the same time every day. For levothyroxine, take on an empty stomach with water. Get regular thyroid function tests as recommended by your physician. Maintain a balanced diet with adequate iodine intake from iodized salt, seafood, and dairy. Manage stress through relaxation techniques as stress can affect thyroid function. Exercise regularly to help manage weight and improve energy levels. Avoid self-medicating with thyroid supplements or herbal remedies. Inform all healthcare providers about your thyroid condition and medications.

## Prevention

Prevention of autoimmune thyroid disease is not currently possible. However, adequate iodine intake through iodized salt prevents iodine-deficiency goiter and hypothyroidism. Regular thyroid screening is recommended for high-risk individuals including women over 35, pregnant women, those with family history, and individuals with autoimmune conditions. Avoid unnecessary radiation exposure to the neck area. Monitor thyroid function when taking medications known to affect thyroid.

## Possible Complications

Untreated hypothyroidism can lead to myxedema coma, a life-threatening condition with severe hypothermia, altered consciousness, and organ failure. Cardiovascular complications include elevated cholesterol, atherosclerosis, and heart failure. Reproductive complications include infertility, miscarriage, and pregnancy complications. Untreated hyperthyroidism can lead to thyroid storm, a life-threatening emergency with extremely high fever, rapid heart rate, and organ failure. Atrial fibrillation and heart failure are serious cardiac complications. Osteoporosis from accelerated bone loss occurs with long-standing hyperthyroidism. Graves' ophthalmopathy can cause vision-threatening eye complications.

## When to Seek Medical Care

Visit your physician at Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital if you experience symptoms of thyroid dysfunction, if you notice a lump or swelling in your neck, during pregnancy for thyroid screening, for routine thyroid monitoring if you are on thyroid medication, or if your symptoms change despite treatment.

## Emergency Warning Signs

Visit the Emergency Department at Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital immediately if you experience extremely high fever with rapid heart rate, confusion or altered consciousness, severe dehydration, chest pain or irregular heartbeat, difficulty breathing, or seizures. These may indicate thyroid storm or myxedema coma requiring immediate intensive care.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Can thyroid disorders be cured?**
A: Autoimmune thyroid conditions like Hashimoto's and Graves' disease are chronic but manageable. Iodine-deficiency hypothyroidism can be reversed with iodine supplementation.

**Q: Will I need thyroid medicine for life?**
A: Most patients with hypothyroidism need lifelong levothyroxine. Some cases of thyroiditis may be temporary.

**Q: Can I get pregnant with thyroid disease?**
A: Yes, with proper thyroid control under physician supervision. Thyroid function should be monitored closely during pregnancy.

**Q: Should I avoid iodine if I have thyroid disease?**
A: Not necessarily. Discuss iodine intake with your doctor. Both deficiency and excess can worsen thyroid conditions.

**Q: Can thyroid problems cause weight gain?**
A: Yes, hypothyroidism slows metabolism causing weight gain. Proper treatment helps normalize weight. However, not all weight gain is thyroid-related.

**Q: How often should I check my thyroid levels?**
A: Every 6 to 12 months if stable, more frequently during dose adjustments, pregnancy, or if symptoms change.
