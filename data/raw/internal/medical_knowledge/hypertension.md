# Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital - Hypertension Knowledge Document

## Overview

Hypertension, commonly known as high blood pressure, is a chronic medical condition in which the blood pressure in the arteries is persistently elevated. Blood pressure is measured in millimeters of mercury (mmHg) and recorded as two numbers. The systolic pressure represents the force when the heart beats, and the diastolic pressure represents the force when the heart rests between beats. Normal blood pressure is below 120/80 mmHg. Hypertension is diagnosed when readings are consistently at or above 140/90 mmHg.

Hypertension is often called the silent killer because it typically produces no symptoms until significant organ damage has occurred. It is one of the most important modifiable risk factors for cardiovascular disease, stroke, kidney disease, and premature death. According to Indian health surveys, approximately one in three adults in urban areas has hypertension, with many remaining undiagnosed and untreated. Early detection through regular screening and effective management can prevent serious complications and improve quality of life.

## Causes

Primary or essential hypertension accounts for approximately 90 to 95% of all cases and develops gradually over many years without a single identifiable cause. It results from a complex interaction of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors including sodium sensitivity, sympathetic nervous system overactivity, and dysfunction of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.

Secondary hypertension accounts for 5 to 10% of cases and is caused by an underlying condition. Common causes include chronic kidney disease, adrenal gland disorders, thyroid dysfunction, obstructive sleep apnea, certain medications including oral contraceptives and NSAIDs, coarctation of the aorta, and pregnancy-related hypertension.

## Risk Factors

- Age above 40 years with increasing prevalence in older adults
- Family history of hypertension in first-degree relatives
- Being overweight or obese with BMI above 25
- Excessive sodium intake from processed and packaged foods
- Low potassium intake from insufficient fruit and vegetable consumption
- Sedentary lifestyle with minimal physical activity
- Excessive alcohol consumption
- Tobacco use and smoking
- Chronic stress and poor sleep quality
- Diabetes and chronic kidney disease
- Indian and South Asian ethnicity

## Symptoms

Most people with hypertension have no symptoms at all. Some individuals may experience early morning headaches, dizziness, shortness of breath, nosebleeds, flushing, visual changes, or chest palpitations. These symptoms are not specific to hypertension and usually occur when blood pressure has been elevated for a prolonged period or has reached dangerously high levels. The absence of symptoms does not mean blood pressure is controlled. Regular monitoring is essential for diagnosis and management.

## Diagnosis

Hypertension is diagnosed when blood pressure readings are consistently at or above 140/90 mmHg on two or more separate occasions. Blood pressure should be measured using a validated device after the patient has been seated and resting for at least 5 minutes. Multiple readings should be taken and averaged.

Classification includes normal blood pressure below 120/80 mmHg, elevated blood pressure between 120 to 129 and below 80 mmHg, Stage 1 hypertension between 130 to 139 or 80 to 89 mmHg, and Stage 2 hypertension at 140 or above or 90 or above mmHg.

Additional diagnostic tests include complete blood count, renal function tests, lipid profile, fasting glucose and HbA1c, urinalysis for proteinuria, ECG for cardiac assessment, echocardiography if cardiac involvement is suspected, and 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring for white-coat hypertension assessment.

## Treatment Options

Treatment goals aim to reduce blood pressure below 140/90 mmHg for most adults and below 130/80 mmHg for patients with diabetes, kidney disease, or high cardiovascular risk. Treatment begins with lifestyle modifications for all patients with Stage 1 hypertension. Pharmacological therapy is initiated when lifestyle changes are insufficient or when Stage 2 hypertension is diagnosed.

Combination therapy using two or more antihypertensive agents from different classes is often necessary for adequate blood pressure control. Treatment is individualized based on age, comorbidities, ethnicity, and patient preference.

## Medications Commonly Used

**First-Line Antihypertensive Classes**:

- **ACE Inhibitors (Enalapril, Ramipril, Lisinopril)**: Block angiotensin-converting enzyme to relax blood vessels. Effective in patients with diabetes and kidney disease. May cause dry cough as a side effect.

- **ARBs (Losartan, Telmisartan, Valsartan)**: Block angiotensin II receptors with similar benefits to ACE inhibitors but without the cough. Preferred alternative when ACE inhibitors are not tolerated.

- **Calcium Channel Blockers (Amlodipine, Nifedipine)**: Relax blood vessel walls by blocking calcium entry into smooth muscle. Effective in elderly patients and those with isolated systolic hypertension. May cause ankle swelling.

- **Diuretics (Hydrochlorothiazide, Chlorthalidone, Indapamide)**: Help kidneys remove excess sodium and water to reduce blood volume. Effective first-line agents, especially in salt-sensitive hypertension.

**Second-Line Agents**:
- Beta-Blockers (Metoprolol, Atenolol): Reduce heart rate and cardiac output
- Alpha-Blockers (Prazosin): Relax blood vessel walls
- Central-Acting Agents (Clonidine): Reduce sympathetic nervous system activity
- Direct Vasodilators (Hydralazine): Directly relax blood vessel walls

## Lifestyle Management

Reduce sodium intake to less than 5 grams per day by limiting processed foods, pickles, papad, and added table salt. Follow the DASH diet emphasizing fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy while reducing saturated fat and cholesterol. Exercise for at least 30 minutes of moderate-intensity activity most days of the week. Achieve and maintain a healthy BMI between 18.5 to 24.9 kg/m². Limit alcohol to no more than 2 drinks per day for men and 1 drink per day for women. Quit smoking completely as it dramatically increases cardiovascular risk. Manage stress through yoga, meditation, or deep breathing exercises. Ensure 7 to 8 hours of quality sleep each night. Monitor blood pressure at home regularly and maintain a log for your physician.

## Prevention

Prevention strategies include maintaining a healthy weight through balanced nutrition and regular exercise, limiting sodium intake, eating potassium-rich foods like bananas and spinach, limiting alcohol consumption, quitting tobacco, managing stress effectively, getting regular blood pressure screenings especially after age 30, and addressing other cardiovascular risk factors including diabetes and high cholesterol.

## Possible Complications

Uncontrolled hypertension damages blood vessels and organs throughout the body over time. Heart complications include coronary artery disease, heart failure, left ventricular hypertrophy, and atrial fibrillation. Brain complications include ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, transient ischemic attacks, vascular dementia, and cognitive decline. Kidney complications include chronic kidney disease, kidney failure requiring dialysis, and proteinuria. Eye complications include hypertensive retinopathy leading to vision loss. Vascular complications include peripheral arterial disease, aortic aneurysm, and aortic dissection. Hypertensive emergency with severely elevated blood pressure can cause acute organ damage requiring immediate treatment.

## When to Seek Medical Care

Visit your physician at Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital for routine blood pressure monitoring at least once a year after age 30 or more frequently if you have risk factors. Seek medical attention if home blood pressure readings are consistently above target, if you experience persistent headaches or dizziness, if you notice changes in vision, or if you develop swelling in legs or feet.

## Emergency Warning Signs

Visit the Emergency Department at Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital immediately if you experience blood pressure above 180/120 mmHg, severe headache with confusion or altered consciousness, chest pain or tightness, sudden weakness or numbness on one side of the body, difficulty speaking or understanding speech, severe shortness of breath, sudden vision loss or changes, or seizures. These may indicate a hypertensive emergency requiring immediate intervention to prevent organ damage.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Can high blood pressure be cured?**
A: Primary hypertension cannot be cured but can be effectively controlled with medication and lifestyle changes. Some patients with secondary hypertension may be cured by treating the underlying cause.

**Q: Will I need to take medicine forever?**
A: Most patients require lifelong medication. Some patients who make significant lifestyle changes may be able to reduce or stop medication under medical supervision.

**Q: Can I exercise with high blood pressure?**
A: Yes, regular exercise is one of the best ways to lower blood pressure. Start with moderate activities like walking and increase gradually. Avoid heavy weightlifting without medical clearance.

**Q: Does high blood pressure run in families?**
A: Yes, genetics play a significant role. If your parents had hypertension, you have a higher risk and should be screened regularly.

**Q: Is white-coat hypertension dangerous?**
A: White-coat hypertension, where blood pressure rises only in medical settings, requires monitoring as some patients may develop sustained hypertension over time.

**Q: Can I take painkillers with blood pressure medicine?**
A: Some painkillers like NSAIDs can raise blood pressure. Always consult your doctor before taking any over-the-counter medications.
