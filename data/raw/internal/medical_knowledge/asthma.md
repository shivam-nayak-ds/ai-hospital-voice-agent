# Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital - Asthma Knowledge Document

## Overview

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disease of the airways characterized by recurring episodes of wheezing, breathlessness, chest tightness, and coughing. These symptoms occur due to inflammation and narrowing of the airways, excess mucus production, and increased sensitivity of the airway lining. Asthma affects people of all ages but often begins in childhood. The severity ranges from mild intermittent symptoms to severe persistent disease that significantly impacts daily life.

India has an estimated 15 to 20 million people affected by asthma. Environmental factors including air pollution, industrialization, and changing lifestyles contribute to the rising prevalence. While asthma cannot be cured, it can be effectively managed with proper treatment, allowing patients to lead active and normal lives. The key to good asthma control is understanding the condition, avoiding triggers, using medications correctly, and maintaining regular follow-up with your physician.

## Causes

Asthma results from a combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental exposure. The airways of people with asthma are chronically inflamed and hyperresponsive to various triggers. When exposed to triggers, the airway muscles tighten (bronchospasm), the lining swells due to inflammation, and excess mucus is produced, all of which narrow the airways and make breathing difficult.

Allergic asthma is the most common type, triggered by allergens such as dust mites, pollen, mold, pet dander, and cockroach droppings. Non-allergic asthma is triggered by exercise, cold air, infections, smoke, strong odors, and stress. Occupational asthma results from workplace exposure to chemicals, dust, or fumes. Aspirin-induced asthma occurs in some individuals who react to aspirin or NSAIDs.

## Risk Factors

- Family history of asthma or allergic conditions like eczema and allergic rhinitis
- Personal history of allergic conditions (atopic dermatitis, allergic rhinitis)
- Exposure to tobacco smoke during pregnancy or early childhood
- Living in urban areas with high air pollution levels
- Occupational exposure to industrial chemicals or dust
- Respiratory infections during early childhood
- Obesity with BMI above 30
- Low birth weight or premature birth
- Exposure to indoor air pollutants including cooking smoke

## Symptoms

Asthma symptoms vary in frequency and severity. Common symptoms include wheezing, a whistling sound when breathing especially during exhalation. Persistent cough, particularly at night or early morning, is a hallmark symptom. Shortness of breath during physical activity or at rest occurs during exacerbations. Chest tightness or feeling of pressure in the chest is common. Symptoms often worsen with viral infections, exposure to allergens, cold air, exercise, or emotional stress. Some patients experience only cough as a symptom, known as cough-variant asthma.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis begins with a detailed medical history and physical examination focusing on symptom patterns, triggers, family history, and response to previous treatments. Spirometry is the gold standard for asthma diagnosis, measuring the volume and speed of air during forced breathing. A reduction in FEV1 (forced expiratory volume in one second) that improves significantly after bronchodilator use confirms airway obstruction consistent with asthma.

Peak expiratory flow monitoring using a peak flow meter helps assess airway function at home. Fractional exhaled nitric oxide measurement indicates airway inflammation levels. Chest X-ray helps rule out other conditions. Allergy testing through skin prick tests or serum-specific IgE identifies allergic triggers. Methacholine challenge testing is used when spirometry is normal but asthma is still suspected.

## Treatment Options

Asthma treatment follows a stepwise approach based on symptom severity and control level. The goal is to achieve and maintain good control with minimal symptoms, no limitations on activity, normal lung function, and minimal use of rescue medications.

**Controller Medications** (taken daily for long-term control):
- Inhaled corticosteroids (Budesonide, Fluticasone, Beclomethasone): Reduce airway inflammation and are the most effective long-term controllers
- Long-acting beta-agonists (Salmeterol, Formoterol): Used in combination with inhaled corticosteroids to keep airways open
- Leukotriene receptor antagonists (Montelukast): Block inflammatory chemicals and are available in tablet form
- Long-acting muscarinic antagonists (Tiotropium): Add-on therapy for poorly controlled asthma
- Biologic therapies (Omalizumab, Mepolizumab): For severe uncontrolled asthma

**Reliever Medications** (used as needed for quick symptom relief):
- Short-acting beta-agonists (Salbutamol, Levosalbutamol): Provide rapid bronchodilation within minutes
- Short-acting muscarinic antagonists (Ipratropium): Additional bronchodilator for acute episodes
- Oral corticosteroids (Prednisolone): Short courses for severe exacerbations

## Medications Commonly Used

Inhaled medications are the cornerstone of asthma treatment as they deliver medicine directly to the airways with fewer systemic side effects. Proper inhaler technique is critical for effectiveness. Metered-dose inhalers require coordination between pressing and breathing. Dry powder inhalers require a forceful deep breath. Spacer devices should be used with metered-dose inhalers to improve drug delivery and reduce side effects. Nebulizers are used for severe attacks or in children who cannot use inhalers. Your physician at Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital will demonstrate proper inhaler technique during your consultation.

## Lifestyle Management

Identify and avoid personal asthma triggers through a symptom diary. Maintain a clean home environment by reducing dust mites through regular washing of bedding, using allergen-proof covers, and minimizing carpet and heavy curtains. Avoid tobacco smoke completely including secondhand smoke. Use air purifiers if air quality is poor. Exercise regularly but use reliever inhaler before exercise if needed. Maintain a healthy weight as obesity worsens asthma. Practice breathing exercises to improve lung function. Get annual influenza vaccination to prevent respiratory infections. Manage stress and anxiety as emotional factors can trigger symptoms.

## Prevention

Prevention of asthma attacks focuses on avoiding known triggers, taking controller medications regularly as prescribed, having an asthma action plan, monitoring symptoms and peak flow readings, getting vaccinated against influenza and pneumonia, avoiding respiratory infections through hand hygiene, treating allergies and nasal conditions, and maintaining regular follow-up appointments with your physician.

## Possible Complications

Poorly controlled asthma can lead to several complications. Airway remodeling with permanent structural changes in the airways occurs with chronic uncontrolled inflammation. Frequent emergency visits and hospitalizations disrupt daily life. Sleep disturbance from nocturnal symptoms affects quality of life. Exercise limitation impacts physical fitness and mental health. Side effects from frequent oral steroid use include weight gain, diabetes, osteoporosis, and immune suppression. Severe asthma attacks can be life-threatening requiring emergency intervention and mechanical ventilation.

## When to Seek Medical Care

Visit your physician at Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital for regular asthma review every 3 to 6 months. Seek medical attention if symptoms are occurring more frequently, if reliever inhaler use is increasing, if symptoms are waking you at night, if you are unable to perform normal activities, or if peak flow readings are consistently below your personal best.

## Emergency Warning Signs

Visit the Emergency Department at Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital immediately if you experience severe breathlessness preventing you from speaking in full sentences, reliever inhaler providing no relief, bluish discoloration of lips or fingernails, rapid breathing with chest retractions, confusion or drowsiness, peak flow reading below 50% of personal best, or feeling of impending doom. A severe asthma attack is a medical emergency requiring immediate treatment with oxygen, nebulized bronchodilators, and systemic corticosteroids.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Can asthma be outgrown?**
A: Some children may experience fewer symptoms as they grow older, but airway sensitivity often persists. Regular monitoring is advised throughout life.

**Q: Is it safe to exercise with asthma?**
A: Yes, regular exercise is beneficial. Warm up before activity and use reliever inhaler if prescribed before exercise. Swimming is particularly good for asthma patients.

**Q: Can I stop controller medicine if I feel fine?**
A: No, controller medicines must be taken daily even when you feel well. They prevent inflammation and attacks. Stopping them increases the risk of flare-ups.

**Q: Are steroid inhalers safe for long-term use?**
A: Inhaled corticosteroids at recommended doses are safe for long-term use. Rinse your mouth after each use to prevent oral thrush. Benefits far outweigh the minimal risks.

**Q: Can asthma medications affect pregnancy?**
A: Uncontrolled asthma is more dangerous to the baby than medications. Most asthma inhalers are safe during pregnancy. Consult your doctor for a pregnancy-safe treatment plan.

**Q: Does weather affect asthma?**
A: Yes, cold air, humidity changes, and high pollution days can trigger symptoms. Cover your nose and mouth with a scarf in cold weather and avoid outdoor activities on high pollution days.
