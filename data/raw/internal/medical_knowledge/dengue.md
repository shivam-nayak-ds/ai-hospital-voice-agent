# Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital - Dengue Fever Knowledge Document

## Overview

Dengue fever is a mosquito-borne viral infection caused by the dengue virus, which belongs to the Flavivirus family. It is transmitted to humans through the bite of infected female Aedes aegypti mosquitoes, which are most active during early morning and late afternoon hours. Dengue is endemic in tropical and subtropical regions including India, where outbreaks are common during and after the monsoon season.

There are four distinct serotypes of the dengue virus (DENV-1 through DENV-4). Infection with one serotype provides lifelong immunity to that serotype but only temporary cross-immunity to the others. Subsequent infection with a different serotype increases the risk of severe dengue due to a phenomenon called antibody-dependent enhancement.

Most dengue infections are mild and self-limiting, presenting as a flu-like illness. However, a small percentage progress to severe dengue, characterized by plasma leakage, severe bleeding, and organ impairment, which can be life-threatening without prompt medical treatment. Madhya Pradesh, including Indore, experiences seasonal dengue outbreaks particularly during the monsoon and post-monsoon months from July to November.

## Causes

Dengue is caused by the dengue virus transmitted through the bite of Aedes aegypti and, to a lesser extent, Aedes albopictus mosquitoes. These mosquitoes breed in stagnant water found in containers, flower pots, discarded tires, coolers, and water storage tanks. The virus replicates in the mosquito after feeding on an infected person during the viremic phase and is transmitted to another person during subsequent bites. The incubation period from mosquito bite to symptom onset is typically 4 to 10 days. Dengue is not spread directly from person to person through contact or respiratory droplets.

## Risk Factors

- Living in or traveling to dengue-endemic areas during monsoon season
- Residence in urban or semi-urban areas with poor waste management
- Presence of stagnant water around living areas
- Previous dengue infection increasing risk of severe dengue upon reinfection
- Age extremes with infants and elderly at higher risk of severe disease
- Chronic conditions including diabetes, hypertension, and kidney disease
- Immunocompromised individuals
- Lack of protective clothing or mosquito repellent use during peak mosquito hours

## Symptoms

Dengue symptoms typically appear 4 to 10 days after the mosquito bite. Mild dengue presents with high fever often reaching 104°F or higher, severe headache particularly behind the eyes, muscle and joint pain earning it the name breakbone fever, nausea and vomiting, skin rash appearing 2 to 5 days after fever onset, fatigue, and mild bleeding from the nose or gums.

Warning signs of severe dengue usually appear 24 to 48 hours after the fever subsides and include severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, fluid accumulation in the abdomen or chest, bleeding from gums or nose, blood in vomit or stool, fatigue and restlessness, rapid drop in platelet count, and skin bruising.

## Diagnosis

Clinical diagnosis based on symptoms combined with laboratory confirmation is essential. Complete blood count reveals characteristic findings including thrombocytopenia (low platelet count below 100,000 per microliter), leukopenia (low white blood cell count), and hemoconcentration indicating plasma leakage.

NS1 antigen test is positive in the early phase of infection, typically within the first 5 days of fever. Dengue IgM and IgG antibody tests become positive after day 5 and help differentiate primary from secondary infection. IgM indicates recent infection while IgG indicates past infection or secondary infection. RT-PCR testing detects viral genetic material and can identify the specific serotype. The NABL-certified laboratory at Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital provides all dengue diagnostic tests with rapid turnaround times for timely management.

## Treatment Options

There is no specific antiviral treatment for dengue. Management is supportive and focuses on maintaining fluid balance, controlling fever, monitoring for warning signs, and preventing complications. Mild dengue can be managed at home with adequate rest, oral fluid intake, and fever management with paracetamol. Patients must be monitored closely for warning signs that indicate progression to severe dengue.

Patients with warning signs require hospitalization for close monitoring, intravenous fluid therapy, and supportive care. Severe dengue requires intensive care unit management with aggressive fluid resuscitation, blood component transfusions for severe bleeding, management of organ dysfunction, and continuous monitoring of vital signs, hematocrit, and platelet count.

## Medications Commonly Used

- Paracetamol (Acetaminophen) for fever and pain control. Use as directed by physician.
- Intravenous fluids including normal saline and Ringer's lactate for fluid replacement in hospitalized patients
- Platelet transfusion for severe thrombocytopenia with active bleeding
- Packed red blood cell transfusion for significant blood loss
- Fresh frozen plasma for coagulation abnormalities

**Medications to AVOID**:
- Aspirin and all NSAIDs (Ibuprofen, Diclofenac, Naproxen) as they increase bleeding risk by inhibiting platelet function
- Steroids are generally not recommended for dengue management

## Lifestyle Management

During dengue illness, rest completely and avoid strenuous physical activity. Drink plenty of fluids including water, ORS, coconut water, and fruit juices to prevent dehydration. Eat light and easily digestible meals. Monitor temperature regularly and take paracetamol as prescribed. Watch for warning signs and maintain daily blood count monitoring as advised. Avoid aspirin and painkillers other than paracetamol. After recovery, gradually return to normal activities over 1 to 2 weeks. Continue monitoring platelet count until levels normalize.

## Prevention

Mosquito bite prevention is the most effective way to avoid dengue. Use mosquito repellents containing DEET, picaridin, or oil of lemon eucalyptus on exposed skin. Wear long-sleeved clothing and pants during peak mosquito hours. Use mosquito nets while sleeping, especially during daytime naps. Eliminate standing water around your home by regularly emptying and cleaning water storage containers, flower pots, coolers, and pet bowls. Keep surroundings clean and dispose of waste properly. Use window and door screens to prevent mosquito entry. Community awareness and fogging programs during outbreak seasons help reduce mosquito populations.

## Possible Complications

Severe dengue, previously known as dengue hemorrhagic fever and dengue shock syndrome, can lead to life-threatening complications. Plasma leakage causes hemoconcentration, pleural effusion, and ascites. Severe bleeding can occur from the gastrointestinal tract, uterus, or brain. Organ impairment including liver damage (elevated transaminases), myocarditis affecting the heart, encephalitis affecting the brain, and acute kidney injury. Dengue shock syndrome results from severe plasma leakage causing circulatory failure. Secondary bacterial infections may occur during recovery. Post-dengue fatigue syndrome can persist for weeks after acute illness.

## When to Seek Medical Care

Visit your physician at Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital if you develop high fever during dengue season, if fever persists beyond 3 days, if you experience any warning signs of severe dengue, or if platelet count is falling. Daily monitoring of blood counts during the critical phase is essential for early detection of complications.

## Emergency Warning Signs

Visit the Emergency Department at Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital immediately if you experience persistent vomiting preventing oral intake, severe abdominal pain, blood in vomit, stool, or urine, bleeding from gums or nose that does not stop, cold clammy skin with rapid weak pulse, restlessness or altered consciousness, platelet count dropping below 50,000 per microliter, or sudden drop in temperature with worsening condition. These signs indicate progression to severe dengue requiring intensive medical intervention.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: How long does dengue fever last?**
A: Mild dengue fever typically lasts 5 to 7 days. Recovery may take 1 to 2 weeks with lingering fatigue.

**Q: Can dengue occur more than once?**
A: Yes, since there are four serotypes, you can get dengue up to four times. Second infections carry a higher risk of severe dengue.

**Q: What should I eat during dengue?**
A: Eat light, easily digestible food including dal, rice, khichdi, soups, fruits, and drink plenty of fluids like water, ORS, and coconut water.

**Q: Does papaya leaf extract increase platelets?**
A: Some studies suggest papaya leaf extract may help increase platelet count, but it should not replace medical treatment. Consult your doctor before use.

**Q: When can I return to work after dengue?**
A: Most patients can return to work after fever subsides and platelet count stabilizes, typically 1 to 2 weeks. Avoid strenuous activity for an additional week.

**Q: Is there a vaccine for dengue?**
A: A dengue vaccine (Dengvaxia) exists but is recommended only for individuals with confirmed previous dengue infection. Consult your doctor for eligibility.
