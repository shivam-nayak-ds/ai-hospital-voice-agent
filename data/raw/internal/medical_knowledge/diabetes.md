# Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital - Diabetes Knowledge Document

## Overview

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by elevated blood glucose levels resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. The hormone insulin, produced by the pancreas, is essential for glucose to enter cells and be used for energy. When this process is disrupted, glucose accumulates in the bloodstream leading to hyperglycemia.

There are several types of diabetes. Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune condition where the immune system destroys insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas. Type 2 diabetes, the most common form, occurs when the body becomes resistant to insulin or the pancreas fails to produce enough insulin. Gestational diabetes develops during pregnancy and usually resolves after delivery but increases the risk of developing Type 2 diabetes later in life. Prediabetes is a condition where blood sugar levels are higher than normal but not yet high enough for a diabetes diagnosis.

India has one of the highest diabetes burdens globally, with Madhya Pradesh reporting increasing prevalence due to urbanization and lifestyle changes. Early detection and management are critical for preventing serious complications affecting the heart, kidneys, eyes, nerves, and blood vessels.

## Causes

Type 1 diabetes is caused by an autoimmune reaction where the body's immune system mistakenly attacks and destroys insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas. The exact trigger is unknown but likely involves genetic and environmental factors including viral infections.

Type 2 diabetes develops due to a combination of insulin resistance and progressive beta cell dysfunction. The body's cells do not respond properly to insulin, and over time, the pancreas cannot produce enough insulin to compensate. Contributing causes include genetic predisposition, obesity, physical inactivity, poor dietary habits, and chronic stress.

Gestational diabetes is caused by hormonal changes during pregnancy that impair insulin function. The placenta produces hormones that can lead to insulin resistance, and in some women, the pancreas cannot produce enough extra insulin to overcome this resistance.

## Risk Factors

- Family history of diabetes in parents or siblings
- Age above 45 years for Type 2 diabetes
- Overweight or obesity, especially abdominal obesity
- Sedentary lifestyle with minimal physical activity
- High blood pressure above 140/90 mmHg
- Abnormal cholesterol levels with high triglycerides or low HDL
- History of gestational diabetes during pregnancy
- Polycystic ovary syndrome in women
- Indian and South Asian ethnicity
- History of cardiovascular disease
- Prediabetes or impaired glucose tolerance

## Symptoms

Common symptoms of diabetes include increased thirst and frequent urination, especially at night. Unexplained weight loss despite normal or increased eating is a hallmark of Type 1 diabetes. Patients may experience increased hunger, fatigue, and blurred vision. Slow healing of cuts and wounds, frequent infections including urinary and skin infections, and tingling or numbness in hands and feet are common. Type 2 diabetes may remain asymptomatic for years, making regular screening essential. Type 1 diabetes typically presents acutely with rapid symptom onset, sometimes with diabetic ketoacidosis.

## Diagnosis

Diabetes is diagnosed through blood tests that measure glucose levels. The fasting blood glucose test measures blood sugar after an overnight fast of at least 8 hours. A fasting glucose level of 126 mg/dL or higher on two separate occasions indicates diabetes. The HbA1c test reflects average blood sugar over the past 2 to 3 months. An HbA1c of 6.5% or higher indicates diabetes. The oral glucose tolerance test measures blood glucose 2 hours after consuming a glucose-rich drink. A level of 200 mg/dL or higher confirms diabetes. Random blood glucose of 200 mg/dL or higher with classic symptoms also confirms diagnosis. Prediabetes is diagnosed with fasting glucose between 100 to 125 mg/dL or HbA1c between 5.7% to 6.4%.

## Treatment Options

Treatment depends on the type of diabetes and individual patient factors. Type 1 diabetes requires lifelong insulin therapy delivered through injections or an insulin pump. Type 2 diabetes management begins with lifestyle modifications including diet and exercise. If lifestyle changes are insufficient, oral medications are added. Insulin therapy may be required in advanced Type 2 diabetes. Gestational diabetes is managed with diet and exercise initially, with insulin added if glucose targets are not met.

Treatment goals include maintaining HbA1c below 7% for most adults, fasting blood glucose between 80 to 130 mg/dL, and post-meal glucose below 180 mg/dL. Individual targets may vary based on age, comorbidities, and hypoglycemia risk.

## Medications Commonly Used

**Oral Medications for Type 2 Diabetes**:
- Metformin: First-line medication that reduces liver glucose production and improves insulin sensitivity
- Sulfonylureas (Glimepiride, Gliclazide): Stimulate pancreas to produce more insulin
- DPP-4 Inhibitors (Sitagliptin, Vildagliptin): Enhance insulin secretion and reduce glucagon
- SGLT2 Inhibitors (Empagliflozin, Dapagliflozin): Help kidneys remove glucose through urine and provide cardiovascular and kidney protection
- GLP-1 Receptor Agonists (Liraglutide, Semaglutide): Injectables that improve insulin secretion and promote weight loss
- Thiazolidinediones (Pioglitazone): Improve insulin sensitivity in muscles and fat tissue

**Insulin Types**:
- Rapid-acting insulin (Insulin Lispro, Aspart): Taken before meals
- Short-acting insulin (Regular Insulin): Taken 30 minutes before meals
- Intermediate-acting insulin (NPH): Provides basal coverage
- Long-acting insulin (Glargine, Detemir): Provides 24-hour basal coverage
- Premixed insulin: Combines two types for convenience

## Lifestyle Management

Physical activity of at least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity exercise such as brisk walking is recommended. Resistance training 2 to 3 times per week improves insulin sensitivity. Weight management targeting 5 to 10% body weight loss significantly improves glucose control. Stress management through yoga, meditation, or deep breathing helps reduce cortisol-driven glucose elevations. Adequate sleep of 7 to 8 hours supports metabolic health. Regular blood glucose monitoring at home using a glucometer helps track treatment effectiveness. Smoking cessation is essential as smoking worsens insulin resistance and cardiovascular risk.

## Prevention

Type 1 diabetes cannot currently be prevented. Type 2 diabetes can be significantly delayed or prevented through maintaining a healthy weight, regular physical activity, a balanced diet rich in fiber and low in processed sugars, limiting alcohol intake, managing blood pressure and cholesterol, and regular screening for individuals with risk factors. The Diabetes Prevention Program study demonstrated that lifestyle interventions reduced Type 2 diabetes risk by 58%.

## Possible Complications

Uncontrolled diabetes can lead to serious complications affecting multiple organ systems. Cardiovascular complications include coronary artery disease, stroke, and peripheral arterial disease. Diabetic nephropathy can progress to chronic kidney disease requiring dialysis. Diabetic retinopathy can lead to vision loss and blindness. Diabetic neuropathy causes numbness, tingling, and pain in extremities. Foot complications including ulcers and infections may lead to amputation. Diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state are acute life-threatening emergencies. Increased susceptibility to infections including urinary tract, skin, and respiratory infections. Poor wound healing and delayed surgical recovery.

## When to Seek Medical Care

Schedule regular follow-up visits every 3 months with your treating physician at Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital. Seek medical attention if blood sugar readings are consistently above the target range, if you develop new symptoms like vision changes or numbness, if wounds are not healing properly, if you experience frequent episodes of low blood sugar, or before planning pregnancy.

## Emergency Warning Signs

Visit the Emergency Department at Lifeline Multi-Speciality Hospital immediately if you experience confusion or difficulty staying awake, rapid deep breathing with fruity-smelling breath, severe abdominal pain with nausea and vomiting, blood sugar below 54 mg/dL with symptoms of shakiness or sweating, blood sugar above 300 mg/dL not responding to medication, loss of consciousness or seizures, or severe dehydration with extreme thirst and dry mouth.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Can diabetes be completely cured?**
A: Type 2 diabetes cannot be fully cured but can be put into remission with significant weight loss, strict diet control, and regular exercise. Type 1 diabetes requires lifelong insulin therapy.

**Q: Can I eat sugar if I have diabetes?**
A: You can eat small amounts of sugar as part of a balanced diet. The key is total carbohydrate management, not just sugar avoidance.

**Q: How often should I check my blood sugar?**
A: Follow your doctor's recommendation. Most patients on insulin check 2 to 4 times daily. Patients on oral medications may check less frequently.

**Q: Is insulin addictive?**
A: No, insulin is not addictive. It is a natural hormone that your body needs. Starting insulin does not mean you failed; it means your body needs additional support.

**Q: Can diabetic patients fast?**
A: Fasting with diabetes requires careful planning. Consult your doctor before fasting to adjust medicines and monitor blood sugar safely.

**Q: Does stress affect blood sugar?**
A: Yes, stress hormones like cortisol raise blood sugar levels. Managing stress through relaxation techniques helps improve glucose control.
